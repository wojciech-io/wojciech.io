Quick-service restaurant growth has different mechanics than B2B SaaS growth. The units of analysis are different (covers, basket size, frequency vs. ARR, NRR, MQL/SQL). The levers are different. The timelines are different.

After working with Burgermeister on digital growth architecture, here's what I understand about QSR growth that transfers — and what SaaS growth operators get wrong when they enter this category.

## The economics are fundamentally different

In SaaS, the growth mandate is often: acquire customers, because LTV far exceeds CAC if you retain them. The acquisition investment pays back over 12–24 months.

In QSR, the unit economics are: the margin on each transaction is thin, the customer acquisition cost via paid media is often higher than the first-visit revenue, and the only way acquisition makes sense is if it drives a customer into a frequency pattern. A customer who comes once and doesn't return is an unprofitable customer.

This means: **QSR growth is frequency optimization, not acquisition optimization.** The most valuable growth investment is converting a once-per-month customer into a once-per-week customer, not acquiring a new once-per-month customer.

## Where the revenue actually comes from

In order of ROI for a mid-size QSR brand:

**1. Local search visibility.** When someone in a 2km radius searches "burger near me," the conversion rate from impression to visit is high because intent is immediate. Investing in Google Business Profile completeness, review volume and recency, and location-accurate data on all map platforms has measurable footfall impact.

**2. Delivery platform management.** Delivery platforms (Glovo, Bolt Food, Pyszne) bring volume but at 25–35% margin cost per order. Managing the platform mix — which platforms to be on, what pricing strategy to use, whether to build a first-party ordering channel — is one of the highest-leverage decisions in QSR digital strategy.

**3. Repeat visit mechanics.** Any loyalty or retention program has to be frictionless. A stamp card app nobody downloads, or a points system nobody understands, doesn't work. The highest-performing repeat mechanics I've seen are: simple push notification with relevant timing ("it's lunch, here's this week's deal"), and order-based rewards with a visible progress indicator.

**4. Menu and basket optimization.** Digital menus can be A/B tested. Photo quality, item sequencing, modifier prompting, and bundle logic all affect average basket size in measurable ways. Most QSR brands have optimized none of this.

## What doesn't transfer from SaaS

**Social media as a primary driver.** Instagram and TikTok can build brand awareness for a QSR brand, but the attribution chain from a post to an incremental visit is long and weak. Social works for brand; it doesn't reliably drive same-day covers.

**Complex analytics setup.** SaaS operators are used to granular attribution: UTM tracking, multi-touch funnels, cohort analysis. In QSR, the primary measurement is footfall and basket data. Fitting a 12-dimension analytics model onto a restaurant chain creates analysis noise, not insight.

**Paid digital as a growth lever.** QSR paid digital (Meta, Google Ads) works for specific campaigns — new location launch, limited-time offers — but ongoing paid acquisition to drive footfall rarely achieves positive ROI. The exception is Google Local campaigns, which can drive measurable footfall lift when the intent targeting is precise.

## The system that worked

The growth architecture that drove compounding results at Burgermeister: local search optimization as the always-on foundation, delivery platform mix management as a quarterly review process, and direct repeat-visit mechanics (push, loyalty) as the frequency driver.

The lesson: know which category you're operating in. QSR growth is retention economics applied to physical footfall. The operators who understand that build the right systems. The ones who apply SaaS playbooks intact build expensive campaigns that don't compound.
