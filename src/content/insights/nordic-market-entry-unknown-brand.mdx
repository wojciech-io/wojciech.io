The standard way to enter the Nordics is a translated site with the local currency bolted on, then the campaigns that already work at home. It is fast. It is cheap. It stalls.

It stalls because a translated storefront is not a local one, and buyers in Denmark, Norway and Sweden read that difference in seconds. Not from your copy. From what is missing around it: the payment method they expect, a returns policy written for their rules, reviews from people in their country, a phone number with the right prefix.

I ran Nordic growth as CMO at Tekhuset for iviSkin and Neatsvor, across SEO, paid, CRO and merchandising. The lesson that cost the most to learn is that the unknown brand does not lose at the ad click. It loses at checkout.

<Callout type="warning" title="Where the money actually leaks">
An unknown brand buys traffic at the same price as a known one and converts it at a fraction of the rate. Entering with paid before the path to purchase works means paying full price to measure your own weakness.
</Callout>

## Trust does not cross the border with you

A brand that is well known in one market arrives in the next one with nothing. Awareness does not cross a border. What crosses is your product, your price and your storefront, and only the storefront is under your control on day one.

Nordic buyers compare more before they commit. That habit is not a barrier if you give them things to compare with: local reviews, clear delivery windows, a returns process that matches what they are used to. It is fatal if the comparison surfaces a competitor who looks local and you do not.

## The order I would run it again

1. **Fix reporting before spending.** Rebuild analytics on margin, not revenue. Ad spend, conversion and margin in one view, per product. Without it you will scale the products that sell fastest and discover later that they were the ones losing money.

2. **Repair the path to purchase.** Checkout, product pages, tracking. This is the cheapest percentage available to a new entrant, and it compounds into every channel you turn on afterwards.

3. **Earn the local search positions.** SEO in a small market is winnable in a way it is not at home, because the competitive set is smaller and often complacent. It takes quarters, so it starts on day one and gets judged on quarter three.

4. **Merchandise for the market, not the catalogue.** What sells in one Nordic country is not what sells in the next. Range, bundles and pricing get set per market, from that market's data, not inherited from the home store.

5. **Buy traffic last.** Once the path converts and reporting tells the truth, paid becomes an accelerator instead of a subsidy for a leaky funnel.

6. **Run storefront experiments continuously.** A market you do not know yet is a market you have to ask questions of. The experiments are the questions.

That sequence moved conversion 25% at Tekhuset while top SERP positions grew across the Nordic stores. The order mattered more than any single tactic in it.

## What transfers to B2B and what does not

This was consumer commerce, so the honest version matters: none of the tactics transfer. A B2B buyer in Oslo does not read a returns policy or a product review.

The mechanic underneath does. That buyer still checks whether anyone local already bought, and still discounts a vendor who looks foreign to their market. They check it through references, case studies and whether your material was written for their regulatory context rather than translated into it. Sequence carries over as well, and it matters more here: fix the conversion path and the reporting before you buy demand, because a B2B entry with a long cycle punishes a leaky funnel for far longer than a DTC one does.

If you are planning an entry and the budget is mostly paid media, that is the moment to move the first third of it into conversion and reporting. The traffic will still be there in a quarter. The money spent buying it into a storefront that does not convert will not.
