Ask an AI to write your landing page and you will have one in ninety seconds. It will be well structured. It will be correctly spelled. It will sound confident.

It will also be about a company nobody described, aimed at a buyer nobody picked, making a claim nobody checked.

That is not a writing problem. Nobody ever told it who you sell to.

## The stack everyone is building right now

The popular version of this looks like a folder of skills. One writes blog posts. One writes social. One does SEO. One does email. One turns a webinar into eight LinkedIn posts. Each is a plain text file you can open and correct, which is genuinely a good idea, and people are getting real work out of it.

I built my own version, and then I put something in front of it that most of these stacks are missing. It is open source and you can install it in two lines:

The rest of this is why the missing piece matters, using one company as an example so it stays concrete.

## What a CMO actually does on a Tuesday

Line up what those marketing skills do: research, writing, social, SEO, email, reporting, repurposing.

Now line up a real Tuesday. You decide which customer segment gets the next three months and which one waits. You kill a campaign three people are attached to. You tell the founder their podcast idea would cost four weeks and explain what it would take those four weeks away from. You decide the company is not chasing the big logo this year. Then, at the end of the quarter, you work out which of those calls was wrong.

None of that is writing. All of it is choosing.

So the honest version is: a good skill folder replaces a marketing operations team, and a fast one. That is worth real money. It is not a CMO. The gap is not that the AI cannot write. It is that nothing anywhere holds the choices, so every skill quietly invents its own version of the strategy each time it runs, and none of them match.

<KeyPoint type="takeaway" title="The failure you will not notice for three months">
A stack with nothing written down does not produce bad writing. It produces good writing about nothing anyone decided to say. That looks like productivity for about a quarter, which is exactly how long it takes to notice that none of it added up to anything.
</KeyPoint>

I have argued the same thing about CRMs: [the entry point is never the tool](/insights/crm-first-ai-adoption/), it is the layer that holds what is true.

## Meet the company

To keep this concrete, here is a company. It is invented. The level of detail is not, and it is the level you need before any of this works.

Ada sells software that shortens month-end close for manufacturers. Her customers have 200 to 2,000 employees, run an ERP older than five years, and sit in Germany, Austria, Switzerland and Poland. She sells to the Head of Operations, IT sits on the committee, and the CFO signs.

Her marketing team is her, two contractors, and now a folder of AI skills.

## The two files

Before Ada's stack is allowed to write anything, it reads two files. They live in the same repository as the work, in plain text, so they can be edited, argued with and tracked over time.

**The first says who she sells to.** Not just the company size, but the trigger: what has to have just happened for this to be worth someone's attention this month. For Ada that is an ERP upgrade, a plant acquisition, or a failed audit in the last six months. Without one of those, the problem is real and the budget is not.

It also says who she turns away, in three lines a machine can apply. Under 200 employees. No ERP, or one less than two years old. Consultancies buying for a client she never meets.

And it holds the only claims her marketing is allowed to make, each with where it came from and when it was checked.

**The second says what she is betting on this quarter.** Two bets, not eight. Each one has a claim, an audience, one number, and a date she stops.

## Watch one request go through

Ada's co-founder sends a message on Tuesday morning: we should start a podcast.

In a normal skill folder, this produces a podcast plan by lunchtime. It will be a good plan. Nobody asked whether a podcast serves either of the two things Ada decided to do this quarter.

Here, the request hits the briefing step first, and the briefing step has to match it to one of the two bets. It cannot. So it does not write the podcast plan. It writes this instead:

<CodeBlock filename="marketing/briefs/2026-09-01-podcast.no-bet.md" lang="markdown" caption="The refusal is written down, dated, and kept. Next quarter, when someone asks again, there is a record of what it was tested against.">
Request: start a podcast

Tested against:
  Bet 1, own the month-end close question  -> no, this is not search demand
  Bet 2, outbound on trigger events        -> no, this reaches nobody with a trigger

Options, in order:
  1. Drop it.
  2. Add it to the not-doing list and revisit in January.
  3. Change the quarter. That means killing or shrinking bet 1 or bet 2
     to pay for it. Bet 1 currently has 12 hours a week and 2,000 EUR
     a month. A podcast would take most of both.
</CodeBlock>

Option three is last on purpose, and it has a price on it. Most requests stop at option one the moment somebody sees what option three costs.

This is the single most useful thing the system does, and the thing that feels worst in the first week. You are used to asking for something and getting it.

## No new numbers, ever

The second rule is shorter. The writing step may only use claims that are already in the first file, with a source and a date.

Any number, customer name, logo, award, or comparison to a named competitor that is not on that list never reaches a draft. If Ada wants to say her customers pass audits more often, she has to put it in the file first, with evidence, by hand.

Most teams do this the other way round: write freely, then catch the invented statistics in review. That does not work. A confident invented number reads exactly like a real one, especially at eleven at night when you are approving the fourth thing that day.

## The number that quietly kills these projects

Here is the part almost nobody plans for.

You promise to approve everything the AI produces. That sounds responsible. Then the AI produces forty things a week and you can genuinely read ten. The other thirty sit in a folder. Within a month you are either rubber-stamping or ignoring the whole thing.

The bottleneck did not go away. It moved to you, and it got a nicer name.

<AnnotatedImage
  src="/images/insights/ai-cmo-ship-gate.webp"
  alt="Three horizontal lanes. The top one runs freely, the middle one has a gate part way along, the bottom one has a gate at the start with work piled up behind it."
  caption="Sort by risk. Internal drafts run free. Public work using approved claims gets spot checked. Anything hard to take back is read every time, and that is where the pile builds."
/>

So sort the work by how much damage it can do.

Internal drafts, research and lists go out on their own. Blog updates and social posts using approved claims get spot checked, one in four, and the whole channel drops to full review if two of the last twenty had problems. Pricing, anything naming a customer, first contact with a named account and paid spend get read every single time.

Then write down the honest number: how many pieces can you actually review in a week. Ada's is six spot checks and three full reads. When the queue gets longer than that, **the system slows down production**. It does not downgrade anything and it does not quietly let the overflow out.

Forty produced into a ten a week review capacity is not forty. It is ten, and a backlog of thirty. Reporting it as forty is the most common lie in this whole category.

## It has to be able to change its own mind

One step is allowed to edit the two files, and only by showing you the change and waiting for a yes.

Once a week it reads what actually happened against the numbers Ada set. Each bet gets one of three answers: on track, off track and here is the specific thing that changes, or stopped. A stop date that arrives is a stop date. It does not get extended because the bet was nearly there.

Once a month it asks the harder question: is any of this still true. Claims older than six months. Rules that never once fired. Language customers use that has drifted away from what the file says. Things Ada swore she would not do this year, quietly done anyway.

Without that loop, this is a content mill with good manners. Same argument as [campaigns plateau and systems compound](/insights/b2b-revenue-system-design-operator-framework/), turned on the AI stack itself.

## What I would not claim for it

It does not send anything. No email goes out, nothing gets published, no ad money moves. The last action is always a person's, deliberately.

It does not replace a CMO. It takes the production work off one, and it turns the strategy into something you can actually read and argue with instead of a slide deck from March.

It will not make a bad plan work. It will make a bad plan obvious, usually inside three weeks, which is faster than most companies find out any other way.

## Questions people asked

### Can an AI agent replace a CMO?

It can replace most of the work a CMO hands to other people: writing, building lists, producing pages, pulling reports. It cannot do the deciding, which is who you sell to, who you turn away, what gets budget this quarter, and what you stop when the numbers do not move. An AI marketing stack with nothing written down is not a CMO. It is a very fast junior team pointed at nothing.

### What is a decision layer in an AI marketing stack?

Two plain files kept in the same repository as the work. One says who you sell to, who you turn away, and which claims you are allowed to make. The other says what you are betting on this quarter, how you will measure it, and the date you stop if it is not working. Every skill that produces anything reads both first and stops if either is missing or out of date.

### How do you stop an AI from inventing statistics about your company?

Give it a list of the claims it is allowed to make, instead of trying to catch invented ones later. Any number, customer name, logo or competitor comparison that is not already on that list never reaches a draft. Checking afterwards does not work, because a confident invented number reads exactly like a real one.

### If a human approves every AI output, does that not just move the bottleneck?

Yes, and that is how most of these projects quietly fail. The answer is to sort work by risk and cap the volume. Low-risk internal work goes out on its own, public work using approved claims gets spot checked, and anything hard to take back gets read every time. Then you write down how many pieces you can genuinely review in a week, and production slows down when the queue gets longer than that.

### How is this different from a folder of good marketing prompts?

A prompt gives you output whenever you ask. This gives you output only when the request matches something you decided to do, and it writes a short note explaining itself when it does not. It also loops: a review step reads your actual results, checks them against the dates you set, and proposes changes to the plan. A prompt library cannot do that, because it has no plan to change.
